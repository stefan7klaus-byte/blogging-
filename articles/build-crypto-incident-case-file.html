How to Build a Clear Case File for a Crypto Asset Investigation

Zenith Hackers Intelligence · Educational guide

A well-organized case file helps a service provider, investigator, legal adviser or law-enforcement officer understand what happened without having to reconstruct the story from scattered messages. Accuracy and source preservation matter more than dramatic conclusions.

1. Write a short incident summary

  • State when the incident occurred, how contact began, what was promised or requested, what you sent and when you realized something was wrong.
  • List the assets, approximate amounts, networks and transaction identifiers. If values are estimates, say so and record the currency and date used.

2. Create a transaction table

  • For each transfer, record the date and time with timezone, network, asset, amount, sending address, destination address and transaction hash.
  • Keep each network separate and avoid assuming that similar-looking addresses on different chains are interchangeable.
  • Use reputable blockchain explorers to preserve the public transaction record and note the URL and capture date.

3. Preserve original evidence

  • Keep original emails, chat exports, payment receipts, exchange records and screenshots. Do not edit originals; make working copies for redaction or annotation.
  • Record website addresses, social handles, phone numbers and case IDs. Avoid publishing private information online.
  • Maintain a simple log of when evidence was collected and by whom, especially if the matter may become legal proceedings.

4. Track reports and next steps

  • Record each exchange, bank, payment provider or authority contacted, the date, case number and any requested follow-up.
  • Separate confirmed facts from assumptions and third-party claims. Note unanswered questions rather than filling gaps with guesses.
  • Never include seed phrases, private keys, passwords or one-time authentication codes in the case file.

Important: No asset recovery outcome is guaranteed. Never share wallet seed phrases, private keys, passwords or one-time codes. This article is general educational information, not legal, financial or law-enforcement advice.
