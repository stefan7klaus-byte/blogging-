Crypto Recovery Scams: How to Avoid Losing Money Twice

Zenith Hackers Intelligence · Educational guide

People who have lost money to a scam are often targeted again. A second scam may promise guaranteed recovery, claim special access to blockchain systems or impersonate a lawyer, regulator, exchange employee or law-enforcement officer.

Common warning signs

  • A promise to recover a fixed percentage or guarantee a result before reviewing evidence.
  • An unsolicited direct message claiming that funds have already been found and can be released after a fee, tax or deposit.
  • Requests for a seed phrase, private key, exchange password, one-time code or remote access to your device.
  • Pressure to act immediately, pay in cryptocurrency or keep the engagement secret.

How to verify a service

  • Independently check the business identity, registration where applicable, professional qualifications, physical contact details and written terms.
  • Contact exchanges, banks and authorities using contact details you find independently—not numbers or links supplied by the person who contacted you.
  • Ask what work will be performed, what evidence supports the proposed approach, what it costs and what the service cannot do.

Protect your wallet and accounts

  • Never share a seed phrase or private key with a recovery agent. Legitimate tracing does not require handing over control of your wallet.
  • Do not install unknown software or allow remote desktop access to someone who contacted you unexpectedly.
  • Keep copies of all communications and report suspected follow-up fraud to the relevant platform and authorities.

Set realistic expectations

  • Blockchain tracing can map observable transactions, but it cannot guarantee control over funds, identify every wallet owner or reverse a confirmed transfer.
  • A legitimate professional should explain uncertainty, costs, risks and possible reporting routes without promising an outcome.

Important: No asset recovery outcome is guaranteed. Never share wallet seed phrases, private keys, passwords or one-time codes. This article is general educational information, not legal, financial or law-enforcement advice.
